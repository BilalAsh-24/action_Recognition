Processing notes

    How It Works

    Acoustic Eye is a web front-end around a phase-based Visual Microphone. When sound waves hit an object, they cause tiny surface vibrations — often far less than one pixel of motion in a video. The algorithm measures those motions and turns them back into an audio signal.

    Pipeline

    1. Read frames — every frame is decoded, down-sampled, converted to grayscale and normalised. Frames that fail to decode are skipped, and the real count of usable frames is what drives every array size (container metadata is never trusted).
    2. Complex steerable pyramid — each frame is decomposed into oriented, band-pass sub-bands (via pyrtools). Each coefficient has a local amplitude and a local phase.
    3. Local phase — local phase is where sub-pixel motion shows up: a small shift of the image shifts the phase of each sub-band.
    4. Phase difference — for every frame and sub-band we compute the phase difference against the first frame (wrapped to (−π, π]). This is paper equation (2).
    5. Amplitude weighting — phase is reliable only where contrast is high, so each phase difference is weighted by the squared local amplitude (equation (3)).
    6. Per-band signal — the amplitude-weighted phase differences are averaged over the whole frame and divided by the total amplitude, giving one number per frame per sub-band.
    7. Band alignment — different sub-bands can be offset in time; each band's signal is cross-correlation-aligned to a reference band (equation (4)).
    8. Signal combination — the aligned per-band signals are summed into a single motion signal (equation (5)).
    9. Filtering & normalisation — a Butterworth high-pass removes slow drift / DC, then the signal is centred and scaled to [−1, 1].
    10. WAV generation — written as 16-bit PCM at a sample rate equal to the video frame rate.
    11. Visualisation — waveform and spectrogram are rendered server-side from the exact signal that was written to the WAV.

    Why the sample rate equals the frame rate

    The method produces one measurement per video frame. That makes the output sample rate equal to the camera frame rate, and by the Nyquist–Shannon theorem the highest frequency that can possibly be represented is half the frame rate. A 30 fps clip can only carry information up to 15 Hz; a 60 fps clip up to 30 Hz. The research results in the original paper used high-speed cameras at 2 kHz–20 kHz. Acoustic Eye does not upsample or invent frequencies the video cannot support, and it labels the Nyquist limit on every result.

    What makes a reconstruction succeed

    • High frame rate (the higher the better — 120/240 fps phone "slo-mo" helps a lot).
    • A loud, sustained sound source close to the object.
    • A lightweight, high-contrast, resonant object (chip bags, foil, paper, leaves, thin plastic).
    • Bright, flicker-free lighting and a rock-steady camera (tripod).
    • Little to no camera noise; longer clips give more signal.

    About & Attribution

    Acoustic Eye is a student engineering project: a web application that wraps an existing open-source Visual Microphone implementation with video validation, a processing API, audio generation, and waveform / spectrogram visualisation.

    Original research

    A. Davis, M. Rubinstein, N. Wadhwa, G. J. Mysore, F. Durand, W. T. Freeman. The Visual Microphone: Passive Recovery of Sound from Video. ACM Transactions on Graphics (Proc. SIGGRAPH), 33(4), 2014.

    Reference implementation adapted

    visual-mic-master — a Python re-implementation of MIT's Visual Microphone by Antonio Musolino and Davide Sforza, released under the MIT License (Copyright © 2020). Depends on OpenCV, SciPy, NumPy and pyrtools (Lab for Computational Vision) for complex steerable pyramids.

    What is ours

    • The Acoustic Eye web UI, FastAPI backend and REST API.
    • Robust video reading with real frame counting and per-frame fault tolerance.
    • Bug fixes to the reference code (see the project README).
    • WAV generation via soundfile, server-side visualisation, job/stage progress reporting, tests and packaging.

    Scientific honesty

    This system attempts to recover acoustic information from tiny visual vibrations. It cannot recover arbitrary audio from an arbitrary video. With ordinary 30–60 fps footage the recoverable band is only a few tens of Hz, so results are typically a low rumble that correlates with the sound rather than intelligible speech or music.
